01
MANTA RAY

Giant Manta Ray

Mobula birostris

The giant manta ray is the largest living ray. It glides through open water using enormous wing-like pectoral fins and feeds by filtering tiny animals from the sea.

HABITAT

Tropical and subtropical open oceans, offshore islands and productive coastal waters

DIET

Zooplankton and other tiny drifting animals

SIZE

Wingspan can reach about 8 metres (26 feet)

FEATURE

Two fleshy cephalic lobes beside its mouth help guide food-rich water inward.

02
MANTA RAY

Reef Manta Ray

Mobula alfredi

The reef manta is smaller than the giant manta and is often seen visiting coral reefs and cleaning stations. It is a graceful filter feeder.

HABITAT

Tropical and subtropical coastal reefs, islands and lagoons in the Indo-Pacific

DIET

Zooplankton and small organisms suspended in seawater

SIZE

Wingspan commonly reaches several metres; smaller than the giant manta

FEATURE

Its dark back and pale underside create a striking contrast; belly markings can help identify individuals.

Read species source ↗ (Wikimedia Commons)
03
EAGLE RAY

Spotted Eagle Ray

Aetobatus narinari

The spotted eagle ray has a dark, diamond-shaped body covered with pale spots. Its pointed pectoral fins make it look as if it is flying underwater.

HABITAT

Warm coastal waters, bays, estuaries and coral reefs

DIET

Clams, oysters, crabs, shrimp, squid and other bottom-dwelling animals

SIZE

Wingspan may reach around 3 metres (10 feet)

FEATURE

It uses plate-like teeth to crush hard-shelled prey and may leap out of the water.

Read species source ↗ (Florida Museum)
04
STINGRAY

Blue-spotted Ribbontail Ray

Taeniura lymma

A small reef ray with bright blue spots across its oval body and blue markings along its tail. It usually hides in crevices or rests on sandy patches near reefs.

HABITAT

Coral reefs and sandy flats in the Indo-Pacific

DIET

Crabs, shrimp, molluscs, worms and small fish

SIZE

Usually about 30–35 centimetres (12–14 inches) across

FEATURE

It has venomous tail spines used for defence, so it should never be touched.

Read species source ↗ (Florida Museum)
05
STINGRAY

Southern Stingray

Hypanus americanus

The southern stingray has a flat, diamond-shaped body, a sandy brown upper surface and a long whip-like tail. It can bury itself in sand for camouflage.

HABITAT

Shallow sandy and muddy bottoms in the western Atlantic and Caribbean

DIET

Crabs, shrimp, worms, molluscs and small fish

SIZE

Disc width can reach roughly 2 metres (about 6.5 feet)

FEATURE

It stirs the seabed with its fins to uncover food and may bury itself under sand.

Read species source ↗ (Florida Museum)
06
EAGLE RAY

Bat Ray

Myliobatis californica

The bat ray has broad triangular pectoral fins that resemble wings. It uses its strong jaws and flat teeth to crush shellfish living in the seabed.

HABITAT

Eastern Pacific coastal waters, bays, estuaries and sandy or muddy bottoms

DIET

Clams, crabs, shrimp, worms and other bottom-dwelling invertebrates

SIZE

Can grow to around 1.8 metres (6 feet) across

FEATURE

It often searches for food by digging into soft seabed sediment.

Read species source ↗ (NOAA Photo Library)
07
EAGLE RAY

Cownose Ray

Rhinoptera bonasus

Cownose rays are social rays often seen swimming in large groups. Their head has two rounded lobes that give the front of the body a cow-nose appearance.

HABITAT

Warm coastal waters, bays and estuaries in the western Atlantic

DIET

Clams, oysters, hard-shelled molluscs and other bottom animals

SIZE

Wingspan can reach around 1 metre (3 feet)

FEATURE

They can form large schools and use crushing tooth plates to feed on shellfish.

Read species source ↗ (Wikimedia Commons)
08
ELECTRIC RAY

Marbled Electric Ray

Torpedo marmorata

The marbled electric ray has a rounded disc and a patterned back. Special electric organs on each side of its head can produce an electric shock to stun prey or discourage predators.

HABITAT

Eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean seabeds, often on sandy or muddy bottoms

DIET

Small fish and bottom-dwelling animals

SIZE

Usually grows to several tens of centimetres; large individuals can approach 1 metre

FEATURE

Unlike stingrays, it relies on electric organs rather than a stinging tail spine for defence and hunting.

Read species source ↗ (Wikimedia Commons)
